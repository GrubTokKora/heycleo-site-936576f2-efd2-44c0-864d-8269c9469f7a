Between grooms

How to care for your dog's coat between visits

Regular professional grooms are the foundation of a healthy coat — but what you do in between makes a real difference. The good news is that a simple home routine takes only a few minutes and your dog will love the attention.

  1. 1

    Brush regularly — and make it a habit

    Most breeds benefit from a brush 2–3 times a week. Long-coated or curly breeds like Poodles, Cavoodles and Shih Tzus need daily attention to prevent matting. Use a brush suited to your dog's coat type and work in the direction of growth, reaching all the way to the skin.

  2. 2

    Check ears, paws and eyes weekly

    After every walk, wipe paws clean and check between the toes for debris or grass seeds. Check ears for redness or odour. A quick weekly once-over catches small issues before they become vet visits.

  3. 3

    Keep nails at a healthy length

    If you can hear your dog's nails clicking on the floor, they're too long. Overgrown nails affect posture and can cause discomfort over time. Our standalone nail trim service starts from $20 — a quick appointment between full grooms.

  4. 4

    Watch for matting before it becomes painful

    Small tangles become tight mats quickly, especially behind ears, under armpits and around collars. Mats pull on the skin and trap moisture. If you feel resistance when brushing, don't force it — bring your dog in and we'll assess the best approach.

  5. 5

    Use the right products for your dog's coat

    Not all shampoos are equal. Human products are never suitable for dogs — they strip the natural oils that protect the skin. Ask us what we recommend for your breed and skin type; we stock a range of Australian-made, dog-safe products in salon.

Pooch Pro Tip

If your dog finds brushing stressful, start with very short sessions and always end on a positive note with a treat. Consistency builds tolerance — and most dogs come to love it once it feels safe.

Grooming schedule

How often should my dog be groomed?

It depends on your dog's breed and coat type. Here's our general guide — but we're always happy to recommend a personalised schedule at your first visit.

Coat type Example breeds Recommended frequency Why
Curly & wavy coats Poodles, Cavoodles, Spaniels, Bichons Every 4–6 weeks These coats mat quickly and don't shed naturally. Regular trims prevent painful tangles.
Long silky coats Shih Tzus, Maltese, Yorkies, Afghan Hounds Every 6–8 weeks Beautiful but high-maintenance. Consistent trims keep coats flowing and mat-free.
Double coats Huskies, Samoyeds, Golden Retrievers, Border Collies Every 6–8 weeks Heavy seasonal shedding; de-shed treatments are especially valuable in spring and autumn.
Wiry & harsh coats Schnauzers, Westies, Border Terriers Every 6–8 weeks Coat texture changes if left too long. Regular breed cuts maintain the characteristic look.
Short smooth coats Beagles, Boxers, Staffys, Labradors Every 8–12 weeks Lower maintenance but still benefit from bathing, nail trims and ear checks.

Not sure where your dog fits? Book an appointment and we'll advise the right schedule for your breed, lifestyle and coat condition.

Quick reference

Good to know

The most common coat care questions — answered simply.

Matted coats

A matted coat doesn't keep your dog warmer — it prevents airflow and traps moisture against the skin, which can lead to irritation and infection. Don't skip winter grooms.

Never shave a double coat

For Huskies, Samoyeds, Golden Retrievers and similar breeds, the undercoat is their natural insulation and sun protection. Shaving causes lasting coat damage. De-shedding is the right approach.

Before the groom

Give your dog a short walk so they arrive calm. Make sure they've had a toilet break. If your dog has any known allergies, skin conditions or health concerns, let us know when you book.

Conditioning treatment

For dogs with dry or sensitive skin — especially common in winter when indoor heating strips moisture from the air — a conditioning treatment at your groom makes a real difference to coat health.
